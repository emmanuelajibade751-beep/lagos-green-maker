Can the fern stay healthy with smaller roots?

Each planter starts with the same wart fern, roots, soil and light. Only the water level changes.

The blue line shows the water level. Soil above it can still be damp. The arrows show possible paths. We have yet to measure where water enters the roots, how much they take up or how long these changes take.

What would we measure? How deep and wide the roots grow, how much root grows, and whether the fern still makes healthy new fronds.

Research behind this

Wart fern (Microsorum scolopendria) has a creeping rhizome and grows in well-drained conditions. We found no verified controlled timeline linking standing water level to root extension or water uptake for this species in our search under Microsorum and Phymatosorus scolopendria. The three stages are hypotheses for testing. NParks species record

  • Roots can reach damp medium above a water table through capillary supply. The reach depends on the medium and its moisture history. Fan et al., rooting-depth study
  • Experimental drying in four Panamanian forests suppressed surface fine-root production. Deeper growth increased at some sites. A deeper tip alone cannot establish an increase in total roots. Cordeiro et al., forest drying experiment
  • Oxygen deprivation inhibited root water transport in Arabidopsis. Wart fern sensitivity and exposure time remain open questions. Tournaire-Roux et al., root hydraulics experiment
  • Uptake depends on root activity and local water availability. A photograph locates visible roots. Establishing where water entered them requires an independent measurement. Root water uptake study
How we’d test it
  1. Randomise independently replicated holders across matched light positions. Match starting rootballs and frond counts. Use the same medium, packing, holder geometry, light and growth period. Include independently repeated, freely drained reference holders kept moist with routine watering. Maintain and log each assigned level. Include unplanted holders at each level.
  2. At fixed intervals log moisture and root-zone oxygen at several depths. Photograph the same covered root windows against a ruler. These images track only the roots visible at the window. Count surviving ferns and healthy new fronds per starting plant at each visit.
  3. At the common harvest, expose each root system in place. Measure its deepest reach below the medium surface and its widest horizontal spread in centimetres before lifting it. Then wash the roots, separate the creeping rhizome, dry the roots to constant mass and weigh them in grams per holder.
  4. Compare depth, spread and dry mass across the three water levels and the freely drained reference. Report variation among holders. A promising treatment produces more compact roots with less root mass while survival and healthy new frond production remain comparable to a thriving reference. Agree on acceptable differences before planting. If the reference struggles or results are too uncertain, the trial is inconclusive. A smaller planter would need its own follow-up fit and health test.

Measure water use separately

  1. Weigh refills and drainage and track changes in stored water. Estimate whole-plant water use from inputs minus drainage, increased storage and evaporation estimated from matched unplanted holders. Canopy shading can change evaporation, so report that uncertainty.
  2. For uptake depth, test a depth-specific water tracer and sample plant water while monitoring tracer movement through the medium. Compare independent holders and report variation.
