Container sizing method

What the numbers mean

External dimensions describe the modelled shell. Usable medium volume excludes shell thickness, base thickness and the top watering gap. Neither external volume nor available medium is a measured root requirement.

For a rectangular bed: medium volume = internal length x internal width x medium depth. For the modelled tapered pot: medium volume = pi x medium depth / 3 x (bottom radius squared + bottom radius x top radius + top radius squared). One cubic metre equals 1,000 litres. The gallery reports calculated litres for each holder. These are geometric capacities, not optimal plant capacities.

How to establish a suitable provision

  1. Identify the species, cultivar, stock size and intended maintained or unpruned crown.
  2. Measure the supplied rootball, including diameter, depth and root condition. Match early pots to that stock; do not choose a very large pot solely to avoid every later transfer.
  3. Specify a drained growing medium and measure its air space, water retention and saturated bulk density. Water storage and aeration depend on the medium as well as the pot.
  4. Trial the candidate volume with the intended plant. Record growth, watering demand, root distribution and signs of restriction; inspect before roots interlock or the plant is stressed.
  5. Confirm whether the next stage stays in place, is potted on, or moves to another bay. Plan access and handling of the actual wet rootball and vessel.
  6. Calculate supported wet mass from medium volume x tested saturated bulk density, plus vessel, plant and any reservoir. Add separate structural and wind checks. Do not assume one litre of medium equals one kilogram.

NC State Extension explains that root restriction can reduce shoot growth, and that both volume and depth matter: https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/extension-gardener-handbook/18-plants-grown-in-containers Smithsonian's Hibiscus guide warns that both oversized and undersized pots complicate watering: https://gardens.si.edu/learn/educational-resources/plant-care-sheets/care-of-hibiscus-rosa-sinensis/

Roselle / research result

A 2019 Malaysian experiment transplanted individual roselle seedlings into 6 L pots, grew them for 127 days, and recorded flowering and fruit production. Mean plant height across treatments was 0.803 m. This proves that the tested plants grew and reproduced in those experimental containers. It does not establish a minimum depth or optimum volume for our roughly 1.57 m developed model, a full calyx harvest in Lagos, or a multi-plant bed.

Source: Shuhaimi, Kanakaraju and Nori, Growth performance of roselle under application of food waste compost and Fe3O4 nanoparticle treatment. https://doi.org/10.1007/s40093-019-00302-x

The rebuilt study uses three plants in a 3.60 x 0.85 x 0.75 m shared bed. The medium is 3.53 x 0.78 x 0.63 m, about 1,735 L total, nominally 578 L per plant if divided equally. This is a generous geometric trial allowance, not a source-required minimum; it carries a substantial wet-load consequence and should be reduced only through a justified root/water provision study. Roots in a shared bed will not divide into equal individual volumes.

The 1.10 m centre spacing leaves room around the saved model crowns. UF/IFAS gives a smaller 0.61-0.91 m spacing for its described roselle, while Rutgers distinguishes closer planting for vegetative leaf production. Neither is a direct prescription for our balcony bed. Use a locally identified crop type and the intended harvest to test the final spacing and provision.

Selected container direction: one drained shared crop bed, maintained through the crop cycle. It meets the requested group-growing arrangement. The best volume remains unproven; no universal pot size is claimed.

Pigeon pea and cassava

Pigeon pea has a deep taproot, but a reported field root length is not a pot-depth formula. ICRISAT supports early nursery transplantation, not unrestricted mature growth in our final pot. Its receiving container is a trial provision. Cassava container culture is documented by NC State. The receiving container is retained to avoid deliberately adding a late transfer of storage roots. The geometry is not a yield guarantee.
